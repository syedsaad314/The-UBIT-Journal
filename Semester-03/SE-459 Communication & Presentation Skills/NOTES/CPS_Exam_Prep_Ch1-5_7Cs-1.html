Good Communication Works

Foundations of the Communication Process
1. Defining Communication & Its Three Forms

Communication is not just talking — it is the transfer of meaning. It is any behaviour, verbal or nonverbal, that is perceived by another person. Knowledge, feelings, or thoughts are encoded by a sender, transmitted, and decoded by a receiver who gives meaning to the message.

Information travels through three primary forms, all essential in a professional setting:

  • Verbal: use of words — spoken (conversations, meetings) or written (letters, emails, manuals).
  • Nonverbal: messages sent by means other than words — facial expressions, body movement, posture, dress, even the layout of a written document.
  • Graphic: representing ideas or relationships visually — shapes, diagrams, lines, charts.
2. The Communication Process — The 7 Elements

Communication is dynamic and interactive, flowing through seven distinct elements:

  1. Sender — encodes an idea into words/signs and transmits it, filtering experience through perception, attitudes, and culture.
  2. Message — the actual idea or feeling transmitted.
  3. Receiver — decodes and interprets the message through their own perception and experience.
  4. Feedback — the receiver's response (intentional or not); gives continuity, shows understanding, stimulates further discussion.
  5. Channel — the means of conveying the message (phone, letter, email, face-to-face). Can be horizontal (between colleagues) or vertical (up/down the chain of command).
  6. Context (Setting) — the surrounding situation; the same message means something different in a boardroom vs. a lunchroom.
  7. Interference (Noise) — anything that interrupts, blocks, or distorts the intended message.
Exam angle: If a question asks "list the remaining six variables besides X," it's testing whether you know all seven by heart.
3. Perception — The "Spectacles" Metaphor

Perception is how people give meaning to their environment — like a pair of spectacles through which we process every signal. These "glasses" can distort the picture, and two people receiving the exact same message can interpret it completely differently.

The 7 filters that colour these spectacles (memorize all seven):

  1. Self-concept
  2. Family
  3. Culture
  4. Skills
  5. Feelings
  6. Attitudes
  7. Values / Morals
4. Barriers to Communication

Barriers occur when a message is misunderstood or misinterpreted. You can't control every aspect of communication — but awareness is the first defence.

Causes: inappropriate channel, wrong choice of words, receiver inattention, mismatched verbal/nonverbal signals, different cultural backgrounds, poor timing.

Strategies to overcome: listen carefully, speak clearly and directly, ask questions, match nonverbal signals to your words, provide structured information.

5. Communication in the Workplace & Balancing Goals

Leaders and managers communicate for three reasons:

  • Control: directing work procedures, encouraging targets. Too much control kills initiative; too little causes insecurity.
  • Motivation: acknowledging achievement, building belonging, improving satisfaction.
  • Balancing needs & goals: aligning organisational goals (profit, efficiency, competitiveness) with individual goals (pay, security, satisfaction).

6. Ethics in Communication

Ethical behaviour means acting with equity, fairness, and impartiality. Ethical communicators follow company policy, communicate objective facts without distortion, and respect everyone's dignity regardless of background.

Four attributes of an ethical business communicator:

  1. Follows company policies and procedures
  2. Communicates factual information objectively
  3. Avoids distortion or misleading information
  4. Respects the dignity of each person irrespective of background
The Ethical Dilemma Checklist (write these exact questions if asked how a communicator evaluates a tricky situation):
  • Is the intended action legal?
  • Is my communication factual and objective?
  • Who will gain, and who will lose from this action?
  • Would I be happy for coworkers, family, and friends to know about this?
  • Am I willing to seek an objective second opinion?
  • Does the language address the real issue, or mask something else?
  • Would I feel confident defending this message at a meeting?
7. Multicultural & Intercultural Communication
  • Intercultural communication: between people living in the same country but from different cultural backgrounds.
  • Cross-cultural communication: between people living in different countries.

Cultures vary in rules of eye contact, naming systems, touching, and use of time. Effective communicators use inclusive language, avoid derogatory labels, and refuse to stereotype.

8. Octavius Black's Communication Behaviour Model + 5 Communication Styles

From the closing case study, Black outlines a 3-stage process for workplace communication:

  1. Identify the Reason: usually to achieve a specific business task or objective.
  2. Interaction: gain full cooperation and support of the people performing the task.
  3. Action/Behaviour: use the most appropriate form (verbal, nonverbal, graphic) to execute the task.

Five broad alternative communication styles:

  1. Instruct — state what is to be done and give essential information.
  2. Inform — communicate what needs to be done with added context.
  3. Consult — get views and input from team members (leader still decides).
  4. Involve — request input that actively influences the final outcome.
  5. Empower — provide a framework and let people do what they think is best.

Black's painting analogy: instinctive communication is like amateur painting — anyone can talk or email, just as anyone can throw paint on canvas. True professional communication means understanding each stage of the process consciously, so you can choose the right channel, minimize noise, and influence others effectively.

Nonverbal Skills

Reading the Message Beyond Words
1. What Is Nonverbal Communication?

Words rarely send the whole message. Nonverbal communication is all parts of a message not encoded in words. It matters because:

  • Actions speak louder than words: nonverbal behaviour is less conscious and reveals true feelings more spontaneously than speech.
  • Contradictions: if words don't match actions, people believe the nonverbal message.
2. Four Types of Nonverbal Messages
  1. Personal: behaviour unique to one individual (e.g. one person works while talking; another needs silence).
  2. Cultural: rule-governed behaviour common to a group, learned unconsciously (e.g. Aboriginal vs. European views on eye contact).
  3. Universal: behaviour common to all humankind showing deep feelings — a smile for happiness, tears for sadness.
  4. Unrelated (Random): behaviour unrelated to the message, like a sneeze — may distract but doesn't change meaning.
3. The Seven Aspects of Nonverbal Communication

1. Body Movement (Kinesic Behaviour) — posture, eye movement, facial expression. Ekman & Friesen divide this into five categories:

Table 2.1 — Categories of Body Movement
CategoryPurpose / Example
EmblemsReplace words entirely — e.g. a nod for "okay"
IllustratorsAccentuate a spoken word — e.g. pointing while saying "over there"
Affective displaysShow emotion, conscious or not — e.g. jaw dropping in surprise
RegulatorsControl conversation flow — e.g. nodding to encourage the speaker to continue
AdaptorsUnconscious responses to inner desires — e.g. scratching an itchy ear

2. Physical Characteristics — body shape, attractiveness, odours, weight, hair. Determine first impressions; can cause attraction or rejection.

3. Touching Behaviour — stroking, hitting, holding; conveys affection, dominance, or equality (like a handshake).

4. Paralanguage (Vocal Qualities) — how something is said, not what. Includes pitch, tempo, rate, and vocalisations (laughing, crying, sighing, "um").

5. Proximity (Space) — physical space communicates feelings/status. Australians typically leave an arm's length for acquaintances, an elbow's length for friends, and close contact for intimates.

6. Artefacts — objects conveying self-concept, image, or mood — clothes, lipstick, glasses, a police badge signalling power.

7. Environment (and Chronemics/Time) — factory layout, desk setup, lighting, temperature. Chronemics (time perception) is critical: punctuality can reflect a casual attitude or a deliberate negative message, depending on culture.

4. Matching Verbal & Nonverbal Messages — 6 Ways
  1. Repeating — pointing when giving directions.
  2. Contradicting — looking at a watch while saying "I'm interested."
  3. Substituting — a facial expression instead of speaking.
  4. Complementing — emphasizing words to convey attitude (e.g. standing casually to show a lack of respect).
  5. Accenting — moving head/hands to emphasize part of the message.
  6. Controlling the Flow — nodding to indicate whose turn it is to speak.
5. How to Check the Meaning of Confusing Nonverbal Behaviour

When the total message confuses you, don't assume. Follow this 4-step process:

  1. Hear the words.
  2. See the nonverbal behaviour.
  3. Confirm the meaning with the sender.
  4. Consider the context.
Crucial rule: describe the behaviour rather than interpreting it. Say "You've just folded your arms" instead of "I see you're in a closed posture." This lets the person explain (e.g. "I'm cold" or "I'm bored") without becoming defensive.

Listening Skills

Hearing Isn't Listening
1. Hearing vs. Listening

Hearing: a physical process — ears receive stimuli and transmit them to the brain (e.g. catching a sudden loud sound).

Listening: an interpretative process involving attention, interpretation, and understanding — including storing, classifying, and labeling information.

2. Four Types of Listening Responses
Listening Response Types
TypePurposeExamples
AttendingShows interest via physical presence & nonverbal cuesEye contact, nodding, open posture, facial expressions
EncouragingInvites the speaker to continue without pressure"I see," "Go on," "Would you care to talk about it?" or brief silences
ReflectiveRestates the feeling and content to show understanding"It sounds like you're really excited about this idea..."
ActiveCommunicates empathy from the speaker's point of view"I understand how you feel. You seem upset about this..."
3. Questioning Techniques & The Listening Funnel
  • Open Questions: start with What, When, Where, How, Who — encourage detail and feeling.
  • Closed Questions: result in yes/no or short factual answers.
  • Probing Questions: narrow the focus, dig deeper into a specific issue.
  • Clarifying Questions: used when confused, or to confirm understanding via an example.
The Listening Funnel: a conversation often starts with open questions to gather a lot of information, then narrows via probing and closed questions to pin down facts.
4. Reflective Listening — Paraphrasing & Mirroring
  • Paraphrasing: rephrasing the content/essential parts of a message in your own words to confirm facts.
  • Mirroring feelings: identifying and restating the emotion behind the words.

Benefits of paraphrasing: helps avoid mistakes, lets the speaker confirm information, and shows the listener is paying attention.

5. Barriers to Effective Listening
  • Listener background: personal prejudices, familiarity with the subject, or the speaker resembling someone disliked.
  • Environmental factors: noise, bad timing, lack of privacy.
  • Communication style: speaker's accent, disorganised messages, rate of speech.
  • Ineffective responses: ordering, judging, blaming, sarcasm (e.g. "Stop it or else," "It's all your fault").

Interpersonal Communication

Trust, Assertion, and Feedback Between People
1. Communication Climate

Communication climate is the tone of a relationship expressed by people's verbal and nonverbal messages — like the weather of a workplace: warm and sunny, or cold and stormy. It's created by how people feel about each other, not by rules or policy.

Positive climate: people feel valued and appreciated; interact confidently and courteously; relationships built on openness, honesty, trust; goodwill; people speak, listen, ask questions, and give feedback freely; information is conveyed accurately. → Communication is open, honest, effective.

Negative climate: contributions aren't appreciated; people feel uncomfortable and unwilling to interact; less willing to ask questions or share ideas; people "wait and see"; communication becomes less accurate. → People shut down, work suffers, errors increase.

Exam tip: given a scenario, ask — do people feel valued? Do they speak up freely? Is communication accurate? That answers "positive or negative climate."
2. Empathy

Empathy is the ability to understand and feel as the other person feels — not sympathy (feeling sorry for someone), but actually stepping into their shoes.

Six skills of an empathetic person (book says five in the question, but lists six — write all six):

  1. Attend to what is said
  2. Retain objectivity
  3. Recognise nonverbal cues about others' feelings
  4. Understand the content of the message
  5. Understand others' feelings
  6. Communicate their understanding back to others

Empathy enhances the communication climate — the tone becomes positive, communication focuses on people's needs and interests, and others feel heard and valued.

3. Win-Win Approach

Concentrates on the needs and interests of the people communicating, rather than winning positions or gaining victories.

  • Gathers/gives information to create a positive climate
  • Transfers information accurately
  • Gets things done — each person understands the other's needs
  • Creates and maintains goodwill; both feel positive about the message

Example: instead of "My way is right," say "Let's look at both our ideas and see which parts work best for the project and for both of us." Nobody loses face.

4. Self-Disclosure

Self-disclosure is revealing how you feel about a situation and something about your past that affects your understanding or response.

Is: sharing ideas/feelings; giving feedback on how others' behaviour affects you; openness from self-acceptance.

Is not: revealing intimate personal details; oversharing.

Climate connection: positive climate → more disclosure; negative climate → less disclosure. Both self-disclosure and feedback increase understanding and open communication.

6. Assertive, Aggressive & Submissive Behaviour

Assertiveness: acknowledges your own rights AND the rights of others. Open, expressive, relaxed. Can negotiate/collaborate comfortably; expresses different views without needing to "win"; wants to be heard, not necessarily agreed with; as a leader, manages without manipulation; as a follower, satisfies both own needs and the organisation's; can disagree and present alternatives without intimidating anyone.

Aggressiveness: feels the need to win at all costs — dominating, sometimes humiliating others; ignoring good suggestions because of who said them; ignoring others' rights to participate; frequently in conflict. "My way or the highway."

Submissiveness: unable to assert a point of view — finds it difficult to lead; submits even against own rights; avoids problems, leaving them to others; can't speak up for itself. Bottles up until it explodes (like Freya in the case study).

7. "I" Statements — The Three Formulae

An "I" message shares emotions and lets others know how you perceive their behaviour and its effect on you. Always begins with "I feel… when…" — it's assertive, not aggressive: it focuses on behaviour, not on blaming.

Contrast: Aggressive ("You" message) — "You make me so angry!" (blames/attacks). Assertive ("I" message) — "I feel angry when..." (owns the feeling, describes behaviour).

The Three "I" Message Formulae (memorize all three)
TypeFormulaExample
Two-partFeelings + Behaviour"I feel angry when the dirty clothes are left on the floor."
Three-partFeelings + Behaviour + Effects"I feel annoyed when you don't let me know you'll be late because I can't reschedule my timetable."
Four-partFeelings + Behaviour + Effects + Acceptable AlternativeSame as above + "I would like you to ring me to let me know if you will be late."

Successful "I" statements communicate non-threateningly; they don't blame or interpret the other's behaviour; they're acceptable to the other person. With practice you can omit the words feel, when, and because and use your own phrasing.

8. Feedback — Definition & Four Types

Feedback is the receiver's response to the sender's message — the continuing/completing link in the communication process, ensuring clarity and mutual understanding since messages are sometimes received differently than sent.

Why it matters: lets the sender know how the message landed; helps the receiver confirm their perception; builds trust; creates a positive climate (a rigid/competitive environment makes people hesitant to communicate).

1. Immediate and Specific Feedback — be specific about behaviour, not general ("This file could do with a tidy up" > "Your files are always untidy"); describe what needs to change, don't judge/threaten; give it only when the person is ready to accept it; refer only to changeable behaviour; don't bring up past grievances — deal with the current situation ("It's your turn" > "You always expect me to answer"); respect the other's right to respond.

2. Negative and Positive Feedback — negative feedback isn't inherently harmful; it depends on delivery. Positive feedback from superiors/peers encourages repeating good behaviour and acknowledges contribution.

3. Informative Feedback — comes from listening to both words and feelings; uses paraphrasing to aid understanding; withhold judgment; try to work out unexpressed feelings; share perceptions rather than giving advice.

4. Undercurrent Message — something you sense the sender wants to conceal or can't convey directly. If you can only sense it without understanding it, it blocks open communication. Skilful feedback brings you closer to understanding verbal, nonverbal, and undercurrent messages — and your own communication style.

Major barrier to good feedback: approving or disapproving another's statements/feelings/attitudes (judging them). Fix: consider their point of view, stop judging, build trust through acknowledging and expressing your own feelings.

Workplace Communication

Instructions, Sequencing, and Oral Reports
1. Giving Instructions — Two Types & The 13 Guidelines

Successful instructions lead to the intended outcome — if the task isn't done correctly, the instruction failed, no matter how clear you thought it was.

Direct Instructions: straight to the point — who, what, when, where, how. Clear Workplace Instructions: explain objectives, background, and intended outcome, then leave the "how/when/where" to the receiver's competence.

The 13 Guidelines for Giving Clear Instructions (near-guaranteed list question):
  1. Determine what has to be accomplished — the intended outcome
  2. Give reasons for doing the job
  3. Follow the steps of the task in the order given
  4. Use verbs (action words), start with a verb — e.g. "Open the file," not "The file needs to be looked at"
  5. Have the person paraphrase instructions back to confirm understanding
  6. Use words appropriate to the receiver's level of understanding
  7. Use familiar, simple words — avoid jargon
  8. Indicate each step clearly, in sequence — no skipping
  9. Demonstrate the skills required for machinery/equipment tasks
  10. Encourage questions
  11. Ensure timing is appropriate
  12. Follow up on progress
  13. Offer timely, specific feedback
2. Structured Approach — Introduction, Body, Conclusion

Introduction: give the purpose; emphasise dangers; highlight safety measures; put the receiver at ease.

Body: outline the whole task first; describe each step slowly; demonstrate then let them practise; check understanding by asking/observing; never assume prior knowledge of standards; use videos/pictures/real objects.

Conclusion: repeat main points; restate standards (especially safety); give constructive feedback; correct mistakes clearly; reinforce what they did well.

Delivery tip: speak clearly, project your voice, use simple words.

3. Sequencing, Timing & Questioning

Three ways to sequence instructions:

  1. Move from simple to complex
  2. Move from familiar to unfamiliar
  3. Follow the required order of tasks

Good timing: instructions given when someone is stressed, distracted, or rushed will fail regardless of wording. Give them at a thoughtful, controlled time so people can listen, share their thoughts, ask questions, and show willingness/ability to follow through.

Questions when giving instructions should check that the person can: remember the facts, rephrase instructions in their own words, and apply instructions to a new situation.

Open vs. Closed Questions
TypeWhat it doesExample
OpenInvites a full, explanatory answer"Can you explain what happens after Step 3?"
ClosedRequires a yes/no or specific answer"Is this step done before or after the safety check?"

Questions when receiving instructions should be: brief and clear, focused on the task, general (for overview) or specific (for facts), rephrased (if the instructor misunderstands your question), open (for more info), or closed (for a definite answer).

4. Variety of Methods & Visual Materials

Range of delivery methods (Figure 5.3): give feedback, coach small groups, give oral instruction one-to-one, ask questions, hang up posters, use surveys to check compliance, present sketches/plans, include instructions in a procedures manual.

Why use visuals? They create interest, add clarity, reinforce main points, show the total picture alongside details, and explain/simplify/emphasise main relationships. Symbols convey meaning across language barriers. Visuals must be simple, clear, and show an accurate picture of what must be done.

5. Receiving & Following Instructions — 8 Strategies
  1. Listen carefully
  2. Concentrate on the instructions, avoid thinking of anything else
  3. Avoid jumping to conclusions
  4. Ask questions about what standards must be reached
  5. Paraphrase to check understanding
  6. Double-check any safety issues
  7. Ask for help if you can't follow the instructions
  8. Ask general questions
6. The Systematic Approach (Figure 5.2) — 6 Steps
  1. Explanation by the instructor — what to do and why
  2. Demonstration by the instructor — show how it's done
  3. Review by the instructor — reinforce key points
  4. Feedback: the receiver paraphrases the instructions back
  5. Observe the receiver use the instructions in the workplace
  6. Feedback by instructor and receiver via questions and answers
Many workplace failures happen because instruction stops at Step 1 — just telling someone — without confirming understanding.
7. Presenting Oral Reports

An oral report is a spoken presentation describing what, where, when, who, and why something happened, using appropriate workplace language.

Six factors affecting the impression given: sentence structure; order of information; knowledge of special terms; appropriate language; status of the audience; cultural factors.

The 5Ws Structure (Figure 5.5)
ElementCovers
WhatWhat happened / is being reported

WhenTime and date context
WhoPeople involved
WhyThe reason / purpose of the report

Planning an oral report: decide its purpose → sort out information → discard the irrelevant → organise into the What/Where/When/Who/Why structure.

Delivery tips: clear logical sequence; state the purpose first; simple direct language; use "first, second, third"; one subject and one action per sentence.

Disadvantages of oral reports: informality (taken less seriously); distortion (as info passes person to person); easier to ignore (no document to refer back to).

The Quality Checklist for Every Business Message

Every business message — written or spoken — should be checked against seven qualities. Expect questions that ask you to define each C, apply it to a scenario, spot violations (wordy vs. concise, insensitive vs. considerate), and rewrite poor examples correctly.

C-I — Completeness

A message is complete when it contains all the facts the reader or listener needs for the reaction you desire. Senders and receivers are both filtered by background, viewpoint, needs, experience, attitude, status, and emotion — so assess your message through the receiver's eyes.

Four benefits of complete messages:

  1. More likely to bring desired results without extra follow-up messages
  2. Build goodwill — show concern for the receiver's needs
  3. Prevent costly lawsuits from missing information
  4. Even small communications can be surprisingly important when complete

Three guidelines (Checklist 2.1):

1. Provide all necessary information — use the Five W's (Who, What, When, Where, Why — plus How). Example: ordering merchandise means stating what you want, when, to whom/where, and how payment will be made.

2. Answer all questions asked — stated AND implied. An incomplete reply looks careless or evasive. If you lack info, say so; if it's unfavourable, be tactful and honest. Book example: a software distributor answered only 4 of 7 buried questions — the incomplete, unfriendly reply cost them a customer's business and goodwill.

3. Give something extra, when desirable — sometimes the reader's own question is inadequate. Book example (Rotary Club): a new member asks only for the meeting location — the complete reply should also add directions, parking, day/date/time, and the program.

Checklist 2.1: 1) Remember the five W's 2) Answer all stated + implied questions 3) Give extra info when desirable.
C-II — Conciseness

Conciseness is saying what you have to say in the fewest possible words without sacrificing the other C qualities — complete without being wordy. "Once is often enough."

Why it matters: saves time/expense; creates emphasis by cutting clutter; paired with you-view, is more interesting; shows respect for the reader's time.

3. Replace wordy conventionsPlease find attached the list you requestedThe list you requested is attached

5. Omit "which/that" clausesShe bought desks that are of the executive typeShe bought executive-type desks
6. Cut prepositional phrasesThe issue of most relevance is teamworkThe most relevant issue is teamwork
7. Limit passive voiceThe total balance due will be found on page 2The balance due is on page 2
Warning: in reducing wordiness, never distort meaning — conciseness is the thoughtful elimination of unnecessary words, not careless cutting.

Guideline 2 — Include only relevant material: stick to the purpose; delete rambling/irrelevant statements; don't repeat what's obvious to the receiver; avoid long intros, excessive adjectives, pompous words, gushy politeness. Example: "We hereby wish to let you know that our company is pleased with the confidence you have reposed in us" → "We appreciate your confidence."

Guideline 3 — Avoid unnecessary repetition (3 ways): 1) use a shorter name after the first full mention (e.g. "North Central" instead of repeating the full company name) 2) use pronouns/initials instead of repeating long names (spell out once with the acronym in parentheses, e.g. "NAFTA") 3) cut needless repeated phrases/sentences by combining them with subordinate clauses.

Checklist 2.2: 1) One word instead of phrases; read aloud to "listen" for wordiness 2) Omit trite expressions 3) Ask "what's really relevant?" 4) Watch for unnecessary repetition.
C-III — Consideration

Consideration means preparing every message with the receiver in mind — put yourself in their place. This is the "you-attitude," empathy, and understanding of human nature — and it underlies all six other C's.

Way 1 — Focus on "you" instead of "I"/"we":

We-AttitudeYou-Attitude
I am delighted to announce we will be extending our hours...You will be able to shop evenings with the extended hours.
We're sure you must be frustrated by the shipping delay...The Polish gargoyles you ordered will reach you within a week.
Caution: "we" can be receiver-oriented if it includes the recipient; "you" can be insensitive in negative situations. Sometimes removing "you" reduces defensiveness:
InsensitiveConsiderate
You failed to enclose your check.The check was not enclosed. (passive, depersonalized)
You are completely off base in your proposal.We have differing interpretations of the proposal's utility.

Way 2 — Show audience benefit: benefits must be real, meet the receiver's needs, and be perceived as benefits by them — not just the word "you" inserted mechanically. Weak: "You will be glad to know we now have a walk-up window open 7–9 AM." Better: "You can now take care of your banking needs at our new Walk-Up Window, open with a capable teller to serve you 7–9 AM and 3–8 PM Monday–Friday."

Way 3 — Emphasize positive, pleasant facts: stress what CAN be done.

NegativePositive
It is impossible to open an account for you today.As soon as your signature card reaches us, we will gladly open an account.
We don't refund if the item is soiled and unsalable.We refund when the returned item is clean and resalable.

Positive words people react to favourably: benefit, cordial, happy, help, generous, loyal, pleasure, thanks, thoughtful. Negative words to avoid: blame, complaint, failed, fault, negligence, regret, reject, trouble, unfair.

Checklist 2.3: 1) See it from the reader's view 2) "You" beats "I/we" in most cases 3) Make benefits prominent 4) Consciously choose positive words.
C-IV — Concreteness

Being specific, definite, and vivid rather than vague and general — using denotative (direct, dictionary) words over connotative (associative) ones.

Guideline 1 — Use specific facts and figures:

VagueConcrete
Student GMAT scores are higher.In 1996 scores averaged 600; by 1997, 610.
She's a brain.Her GPA in 1996 was 3.9 on a four-point scale.

Vague words to avoid: slightly, most, soon, high, almost, small, a few, slow, very, about, several.

Three exceptions — general expressions are OK when: 1) exact figures aren't available 2) you want to be diplomatic ("I've sent you several reminders" vs. "You missed three invitations") 3) exact figures are unimportant ("more than half the committee was present").

Guideline 2 — Put action in your verbs:

Active vs. Passive: subject acts (active) vs. subject receives the action (passive — has a "to be" form + often an implied "by").

PassiveActive
The tests were administered by the professors.Professors administered the tests.

Four reasons active is better: specific, personal, concise, emphatic.

Three situations where passive is preferred: 1) avoiding blunt accusations ("The October check was not included" vs. "You failed to include...") 2) stressing the object of the action ("Your savings account is insured up to $100,000") 3) when the doer isn't important ("Three announcements were made before the meeting started").

Put action in verbs, not nouns: seven "deadly" verbs — be, give, have, hold, make, put, take — hide action in a "quiet" noun. "Professor H. will give consideration to the report" → "Professor H. will consider the report."

Put action in verbs, not infinitives: "It is his responsibility to keep the assignment book up to date" → "He keeps the assignment book up to date."

Guideline 3 — Choose vivid, image-building words: sensory appeal ("Sweat trickled down the arms of the line workers" instead of "It was hot in the factory"); comparisons/analogies ("This letter is three times as long as you said it would be"); figurative language, used sparingly ("She could be called the spark plug of the group").

Checklist 2.4: 1) Precise facts/figures where possible 2) Active over passive voice 3) Action in verbs, not nouns/infinitives 4) Vivid words used sparingly.
C-V — Clarity

Getting the meaning from your head into the reader's head, accurately, is the purpose of clarity.

Guideline 1 — Choose precise, concrete, familiar words: balance precise language with familiar language; use the shorter, familiar word.

FamiliarPretentious

The Plumber Story (classic exam example): a plumber asked if hydrochloric acid is safe for pipes. A technical reply ("The efficacy of hydrochloric acid is indisputable, but the corrosive residue is incompatible with metallic permanence") confused him into thinking they agreed. A manager's plain reply — "Don't use hydrochloric acid. It eats hell out of the pipes." — was instantly clear. Lesson: familiar language beats jargon for clarity.

Guideline 2 — Construct effective sentences and paragraphs (length, unity, coherence, emphasis):

  • Length: average 17–20 words; vary from 3–30+; rewrite anything over 40 words; avoid making everything under 10 words (choppy/primer-like).
  • Unity: one main idea per sentence — "I like Jim, and the Eiffel Tower is in Paris" is NOT unified.
  • Coherence: place modifiers close to what they modify. "Being an excellent lawyer, I am sure you can help us" (unclear) → "Being an excellent lawyer, you can surely help us" (clear).
  • Emphasis: put the main idea in the main clause, not a subordinate one. Visual emphasis tools: headings, tabulation, itemization, graphs, underlining, italics, colored capitals, wide/short margins.
Checklist 2.5: 1) Precise/concrete words 2) High appropriateness for the reader 3) Familiar, not pretentious 4) 17–20 word average sentence length 5) One main idea per sentence 6) Main idea placed early.
C-VI — Courtesy

True courtesy is being aware of others' perspectives AND feelings — not mechanical "please/thank you," but politeness growing from real respect.

Guideline 1 — Be sincerely tactful, thoughtful, appreciative: bluntness usually stems from a mistaken idea of conciseness, negative attitudes, or not knowing the audience's culture.

TactlessTactful
Stupid letter; I can't understand any of it.It's my understanding...
Clearly, you did not read my latest fax.Sometimes my wording isn't precise; let me try again.

An internal email to a colleague can be short and blunt; a letter to a foreign company needs more tactful, softer wording. Book tip: be especially courteous with Asian cultures, who favour a soft, polite approach.

Guideline 2 — Use expressions that show respect: omit irritating expressions, especially with "you/your" — e.g. inexcusable, irresponsible, obviously you overlooked, you failed to, you neglected to, your complaint, your failure to. Also omit questionable humor — "when in doubt, leave it out."

Checklist 2.6: 1) Sincere you-attitude? 2) Have someone else review for tact 3) Be cautious with humor — get a second opinion 4) Watch for discriminatory language (gender, race, age, sexual orientation, ethnicity).

Key Terms — The 7 C's

Conciseness: fewest words without sacrificing quality
Consideration: messages prepared with the receiver in mind — the "you-attitude"
Concreteness: specific, definite, vivid — not vague

Connotative words: ideas/associations suggested by a word
Active/Passive voice: subject performs vs. receives the action
